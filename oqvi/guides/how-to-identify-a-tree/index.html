Beginner method

Identify the tree by building a case.

Good field identification is not a lucky match. It is a sequence of observations that makes the list of possibilities smaller and the uncertainty visible.

Published by Kestora Labs, the maker of Oqvi · Updated November 5, 2026

Start with region and overall form, then move to leaves, twigs, bark and seasonal structures.

Use a regional key to compare the complete evidence. Save the candidate as uncertain until several independent features agree.

The seven-step tree method

  1. Set the regionNative range, elevation, habitat and planted landscapes change what is plausible.
  2. Read the silhouetteNotice crown shape, trunk form, branching angle and whether the tree is evergreen.
  3. Classify the foliageBroad leaves, needles or scales; simple or compound; opposite or alternate.
  4. Describe the barkRecord texture, ridges, furrows, plates, peeling and variation with age.
  5. Inspect twigs and budsWinter buds, leaf scars, pith and twig arrangement remain when leaves are absent.
  6. Find flowers, fruit or conesSeasonal reproductive structures can be decisive.
  7. Confirm with a keyUse a regional dichotomous key or qualified local source and check the final description.

Keep observations separate from conclusions

Write “leaves opposite, margins toothed, bark gray with shallow ridges” before writing a species name. This prevents the first suggestion from changing how you remember the evidence.

Revisit the same tree

A tree becomes easier to identify across seasons. Spring flowers, summer leaves, autumn fruit and winter buds each add independent evidence. Use the same saved discovery so you can compare change rather than creating disconnected guesses.

Know when a professional answer is needed

Ask an arborist, botanist, extension service or herbarium when identification informs treatment, removal, conservation, legal compliance, edibility or toxicity. Oqvi and other photo tools provide suggestions for learning, not professional certification.

FIELD SAFETY

Identification results can be incomplete or wrong. Never rely on a photo or AI answer for edibility, toxicity, medical care, fire safety, structural use or another consequential decision. Confirm with a qualified expert.
