What are the best foods for healthy weight gain?

Choose foods that make the calorie target easier while still contributing protein, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins and minerals. The most useful options fit your appetite, budget, dietary needs and normal meals.

Published November 4, 2026 · Written and reviewed by Kestora Labs · 7 min read

Quick answer

Start with familiar meals, add a protein source, include a starchy carbohydrate, and raise energy with foods such as nuts, seeds, cheese, avocado, olive oil, dairy or suitable alternatives. Use snacks or nourishing drinks when larger portions are difficult.

Add calories to meals you already eat

The NHS suggests adding foods such as cheese, nuts and seeds to meals. NHS dietetic guidance also gives practical examples such as adding olive or rapeseed oil during cooking, using yogurt or cheese in dishes, and combining cereals with milk, fruit, nuts and seeds.

Small additions are easier to repeat than a completely new diet. Add nut butter to toast, olive oil to a rice bowl, cheese to eggs or pasta, or yogurt and nuts beside breakfast. Account for allergies, intolerances and dietary restrictions.

When appetite is the limiting factor

Large meals are not the only route. Eating smaller meals more often, adding two or three nourishing snacks and using higher-calorie drinks between meals can increase intake with less pressure at one sitting. Avoid filling up on drinks immediately before a meal if that reduces appetite.

Do not turn “high calorie” into “anything goes”

The NHS advises against relying on chocolate, cakes and sugary drinks for weight gain. Diet quality still matters. Include fruits and vegetables, vary protein sources, and use a mixture of carbohydrate and fat sources rather than building the entire surplus from one type of food.

The terms “clean” and “dirty” can obscure this. Read clean bulk vs dirty bulk for a clearer way to think about surplus size and food quality.

Match the advice to your health

Generic food lists may be inappropriate for diabetes, kidney disease, digestive conditions, food allergies or other medical needs. Sudden or unintentional weight loss should be assessed rather than treated only by adding calories.
