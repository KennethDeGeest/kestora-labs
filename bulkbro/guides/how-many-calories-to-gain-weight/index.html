Weight-gain calories · Practical guide

How many calories should you eat to gain weight?

Start with an estimate, add a gradual surplus, and let your consistent intake and weight response tell you whether the target is working.

Published November 4, 2026 · Reviewed against NHS, NIDDK, and peer-reviewed sources · 8 min read

Quick answer

Estimate your maintenance calories, then add a gradual daily surplus. The NHS says adults seeking gradual weight gain could try adding around 300–500 calories per day. Treat the result as a starting point and compare it with your actual intake and weight response.

1. Find a starting calorie estimate

Your starting target has two parts:

  1. Estimated maintenance calories: roughly how much energy your current body and activity level may require to maintain weight.
  2. A calorie surplus: additional energy above that estimate to support intentional weight gain.

Equations such as Mifflin–St Jeor estimate resting energy needs from age, height, weight, and a sex-specific coefficient. An activity multiplier is then used to estimate total daily needs. This is useful for choosing a starting number, but it is not a direct measurement of your metabolism.

2. Choose a gradual starting surplus

The NHS suggests that adults trying to gain weight gradually could try adding roughly 300–500 calories per day. It also recommends a balanced diet, protein-containing foods, and strength training or yoga rather than relying on sugary foods and drinks.

Starting surplusHow to treat it
+300 kcal/dayA gradual starting option from the lower end of the NHS example range.
+400 kcal/dayA practical midpoint when you need one initial number.
+500 kcal/dayThe upper end of the NHS example range—not a guarantee of a specific result.

A larger surplus is not automatically better. The International Society of Sports Nutrition notes that the appropriate surplus varies with training status and individual response, and that more advanced trainees may prefer smaller surpluses to limit unwanted fat gain.

3. Track what you actually eat

Your target and your intake are different numbers. If a plan says 3,000 calories but your completed logs average 2,650, the response should be interpreted against 2,650—not the target you intended to hit.

4. Compare the target with your weight response

A formula cannot see what happened after you followed it. Your real progress creates the missing evidence.

BulkBro evaluates a completed seven-day week using your average logged intake, the opening and closing weight for that week, and your selected weekly goal. It can hold a working target steady or make a measured adjustment when the observed response differs from the intended pace.

The useful loop

Estimate → log consistently → complete the week → weigh in → compare → adjust only when the evidence supports it.

Do not confuse one noisy measurement with a trend

Individual weigh-ins can vary. A consistent measurement routine and repeated observations make it easier to judge the direction of change than reacting to one reading.

When to speak with a healthcare professional

Do not use a calorie target to diagnose or treat being underweight, malnutrition, an eating disorder, or another medical condition. The NHS advises speaking with a GP if you have lost weight suddenly or without changing your diet or exercise routine.

Seek qualified guidance before intentional weight gain if you are pregnant, under 18, have a medical condition, take medication that may affect weight or metabolism, or have a history of disordered eating.
