Symptom recording · Observation first

Track what happened—not what a pattern must mean.

A symptom record can preserve timing and context for a healthcare conversation. It should not turn a repeated detail into a diagnosis or proven trigger.

A symptom tracker is a memory aid

Record the symptom, approximate time, duration, severity or impact, and whether the note was made during the episode or recalled later. A qualified professional can interpret that history in context.

Useful symptom categories

You do not need to record every possible symptom. Start with the experiences that matter to you or that a healthcare professional has asked you to track.

  • Pain location
  • Pain description
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Light sensitivity
  • Sound sensitivity
  • Smell sensitivity
  • Visual changes
  • Numbness or tingling
  • Dizziness
  • Speech difficulty
  • Fatigue
  • Concentration changes
  • Functional impact

These labels are not a self-diagnosis checklist. Record unfamiliar, severe or concerning symptoms and seek appropriate medical help rather than relying on an app.

Before, during and after

BeforeEarly changes you noticed and when they began.
Aura, if experiencedDescribe the actual sensory or speech experience and its timing.
Headache phasePain, nausea, sensitivities and impact on usual activity.
AfterLingering fatigue, concentration changes or recovery time.

The American Migraine Foundation describes four broad phases but emphasizes that not everyone experiences every phase and they can overlap. Use the structure only when it matches your own record.

Neutral wording prevents overclaiming

Observation“Light sensitivity began around 13:30.”
Avoid certainty“The screen caused the migraine.”
Observation“Medication recorded at 14:10; relief noted later.”
Avoid treatment claims“This medication always fixes it.”

Do not use a tracker when urgent care is needed

New, sudden or severe symptoms require medical judgment, not more data entry. NHS guidance says to seek emergency help for a sudden extremely painful headache or headache with problems speaking, loss of vision, confusion, seizure, one-sided weakness, a very high temperature with meningitis symptoms, or recent head injury. Emergency guidance varies by country; use the services appropriate to your location.
