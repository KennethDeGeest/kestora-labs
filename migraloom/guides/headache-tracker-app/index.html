Headache records · App guide

A headache tracker app should make the next conversation easier

A useful tracker does not need to explain every headache. Its job is quieter: help you capture what happened, preserve the gaps you honestly do not remember, and turn weeks of scattered moments into something you and a healthcare professional can review.

Published by Kestora Labs, the maker of MigraLoom · Updated November 16, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answerWhat should a headache tracker app do?

It should make episode entry quick, keep frequency and severity visible, record medication and impact without forcing guesses, and produce a clear summary you can bring to an appointment.

Get urgent medical help when appropriate. A tracker is not emergency care. Follow local emergency guidance for a sudden extremely painful headache, headache after serious injury, or headache with new weakness, confusion, seizure, vision loss, fever with a stiff neck or other emergency signs.

Start with the useful minimum

When head pain is active, a long questionnaire is the last thing many people want. A good headache tracker app should let you save the minimum first and add context later. The minimum is usually the date, approximate timing, severity or functional impact, and whether medication was taken.

The American Migraine Foundation recommends recording headache frequency along with location, intensity, quality and associated symptoms such as nausea or sensitivity to light and sound. Medication timing and response can also matter. That does not mean every field must be completed during every episode.

A blank is useful information. It means “not recorded,” not “did not happen.” Apps that silently convert missing answers into symptom-free days can make the history look cleaner than it really is.

Avoid a tracker that turns correlation into certainty

If headaches often follow poor sleep, that pattern may be worth discussing. It does not prove that sleep was the cause. The same is true for food, weather, caffeine, hormones or stress. A tracker should show timing and repetition without declaring a trigger or recommending treatment.

Neutral language helps. “Coffee at 09:00; symptoms around 11:30” preserves what you observed. “Coffee caused the headache” adds a conclusion the record alone cannot establish. Our migraine symptom tracking guide gives more examples of observation-first wording.

Use a three-part workflow

  1. Capture the episode. Save a short entry while the information is fresh. Approximate timing is better than false precision.
  2. Complete it later. Add duration, symptoms, medication response and impact when you have the attention to do so.
  3. Review the month. Look at headache days, medication days and gaps in the record before trying to interpret individual details.

This workflow separates recording from analysis. It also reduces the temptation to fill every field simply because the app presents one.

The report is where the app earns its place

A list of individual episodes can become difficult to scan. Before an appointment, the useful questions are often broader: How many headache days were recorded? How severe were they? Which medications were used, and how often? What changed in function or frequency?

Check whether a tracker can export or present a readable summary without requiring the clinician to learn the app. A report should retain dates and uncertainty, not hide them behind a single “health score.”

Check privacy, access and pricing before committing

Headache records can include sensitive health information. Look for a clear explanation of where data is stored, whether an account is required, how exports work and what happens if you stop paying. A free download does not necessarily mean every useful feature remains free.

A tracker supports care; it does not replace it

Headache and migraine symptoms vary, and a record cannot determine a diagnosis. The NHS notes that keeping a migraine diary may help identify possible patterns, but new, severe or changing symptoms still need appropriate medical assessment.

Bring the record to a qualified healthcare professional rather than changing prescribed treatment based only on an app pattern. If tracking increases anxiety or becomes burdensome, simplify the fields or discuss another approach with your clinician.
