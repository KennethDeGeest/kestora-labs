The smallest useful diary wins

The Migraine Trust recommends keeping a diary simple. The American Migraine Foundation likewise notes that journaling should not feel overwhelming. A date, approximate timing, severity and one short note can be enough to preserve useful context.

What a headache diary is for

A diary can help you remember frequency, duration, severity, symptoms, medication use and functional impact. It can also give a healthcare professional a clearer history than memory alone. It does not prove that one event caused another, and it does not diagnose the type or cause of a headache.

How to keep a headache diary in five steps

  1. Choose one purpose.
    For example: remember episode timing, prepare for an appointment or track how often headaches occurred.
  2. Pick the smallest useful fields.
    Date, approximate start and end, severity, medication taken and one symptom or impact note are a practical core.
  3. Define what blanks mean.
    A blank might mean “not recorded,” not “no headache.” If symptom-free days matter, mark them explicitly.
  4. Record near the event when possible.
    If you complete details later, distinguish recollection from information captured at the time.
  5. Review with questions, not conclusions.
    Notice what you want to discuss without declaring a trigger or diagnosis from a pattern.

A minimum entry for a difficult day

Date · time · severity · one note

Example structure: “Nov 5 · began around 14:00 · 7/10 · light sensitivity; medication recorded at 14:20.” This is an illustrative format, not medical guidance.

You can use our ten-entry headache log, the monthly printable diary, or an app. Choose whichever lets you capture the record with the least friction.

How to review the diary before an appointment

  • Choose the date range your appointment concerns.
  • Count recorded headache days separately from unrecorded days.
  • Note changes in frequency, severity or duration you want to discuss.
  • Bring a current medication list and the questions you do not want to forget.
  • Keep the raw record available so a summary can be checked against it.
